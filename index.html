Hi! I'm Gouri.

I'm Gouripriya S. Menon, an aspiring astrobiologist from Kerala, India. With a background in geology and palaeontology, I'm driven by a deep curiosity about the origins of life on Earth and how that understanding can guide our search for life elsewhere in our solar system and beyond.

I am a PhD student at the University of Copenhagen, Denmark, where I work on improving how we tell biological carbon apart from non-biological carbon in Earth's oldest rocks: the 3.7 to 3.8 billion-year-old Isua Supracrustal Belt in Greenland.

I've always been fascinated by the intersection of philosophy and science, and this space is where I hope to bring together my thoughts, experiences and scientific journey.

Master's Thesis
2025 · Siljan impact crater, Sweden

Bioprospecting for Hydrocarbon-Degrading Microorganisms at the Siljan Impact Crater

Swedish Museum of Natural History, Stockholm · supervised by Dr. Magnus Ivarsson

Impact craters, and the hydrothermal systems they create, may be good places for microbial life, both on Earth and possibly on other planets. My thesis looked at the late Devonian Siljan impact crater in Sweden, a key example of how a crater can support deep microbial life hundreds of millions of years after the impact-driven hydrothermal activity has ended. Siljan is also known for significant microbial methane seepage.

These crater microbial communities are heterotrophic, so they could get their carbon and energy from hydrocarbons moving through the fractures the impact created. To test this in a modern setting, I searched the crater for hydrocarbon-degrading microorganisms, particularly fungi.

Methods

Crude oil was sampled from drill holes in the Solberga quarry and cultured on petri dishes. Pure cultures were identified by Sanger sequencing of the fungal ITS region and by microscopy with calcofluor staining. The redox indicator DCPIP (2,6-dichlorophenol-indophenol) served as a first test of hydrocarbon degradation, followed by hexane-based hydrocarbon extraction and GC-MS for a quantitative check.

Key findings

Seven isolates were identified: Ascochyta phacae, Penicillium pancosmium, Rhodotorula mucilaginosa, Penicillium citreosulfuratum, Absidia glauca, Fusarium salinense and Mucor hiemalis. P. citreosulfuratum performed best in the DCPIP assays, and GC-MS confirmed hydrocarbon degradation in the samples, although some peaks could not be fully resolved.

Why it matters

Siljan fungi can break down the crater's crude oil and feed subsurface carbon cycling: their products could be used by methanogens in the crater to make methane. This supports the idea that impact craters can be long-lived biological systems. Seasonal methane changes detected on Mars suggest this kind of cycling might happen beyond Earth, and that heterotrophic life deserves a place alongside purely abiotic explanations.
